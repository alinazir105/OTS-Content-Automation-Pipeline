Introduction

Earth's Layers

Earth is a unique planet that supports life, thanks to its structure and composition. It is divided into four main layers: the crust, mantle, outer core, and inner core. These layers are distinct in their characteristics, composition, and function.

From the solid crust where we live to the molten metal in the core, each layer plays a crucial role in shaping Earth's surface and processes like earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and mountain formation.

Layer 1

The Crust 🪨

The crust is the outermost layer of the Earth. It is where all life exists, including humans, animals, and plants. The crust is made of solid rock and soil. It is the thinnest layer, yet it plays a vital role in supporting all life on Earth.

📏 Thickness of the Crust

The crust averages about 30 kilometres on land (continental crust) and only 5–10 kilometres under the oceans (oceanic crust).

🏔️

Continental Crust

Made mostly of granite, it forms the continents. It is thicker and less dense than oceanic crust.

📏 Thickness: ~30 km
🌊

Oceanic Crust

Made mostly of basalt, it forms the ocean floor. It is thinner and denser than continental crust.

📏 Thickness: 5–10 km

Layer 2

The Mantle 🔥

Beneath the crust lies the mantle, the thickest layer of the Earth, extending about 2,900 kilometres deep. It is made of semi-solid, hot rocks that move slowly.

This movement is responsible for the shifting of tectonic plates, which can cause earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.

🌋 Fun Fact

The mantle accounts for 84% of Earth's total volume! It is the largest layer by volume.

🧊

Upper Mantle

The part closest to the crust. It is semi-solid and cooler compared to the rest of the mantle. The asthenosphere — a soft, flowing part — is in the upper mantle and allows tectonic plates to move.

🌡️ Cooler, semi-solid
🔴

Lower Mantle

Located deeper inside, the lower mantle is hotter and more fluid than the upper mantle. The extreme heat drives convection currents that push tectonic plates.

🌡️ Hotter, more fluid
🔄 How Mantle Convection Works
1
Heat from the core warms the rock at the bottom of the mantle.
2
Hot rock becomes less dense and rises slowly upward.
3
As it reaches the top, it cools down and becomes denser.
4
Cooled rock sinks back down, completing the convection cycle.
5
This slow circular movement pushes tectonic plates, causing earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.
Layers 3 & 4

The Core ⚙️

At the very centre of the Earth lies the core. It is divided into two distinct parts: the outer core and the inner core, each with very different properties.

💧

Outer Core

Made of liquid iron and nickel. It is about 2,200 kilometres thick. The movement of this liquid metal creates Earth's powerful magnetic field.

🧲 Creates Earth's magnetic field
⚽

Inner Core

A solid ball of iron and nickel, about 1,200 kilometres thick. Despite being the hottest layer (5,000–6,000°C), immense pressure keeps it solid.

☀️ As hot as the Sun's surface!
🔬 Why is the Inner Core Solid?

Even though the inner core reaches temperatures of 5,000–6,000°C — as hot as the surface of the Sun — it remains solid. This is because the enormous pressure from all the layers above squeezes the iron and nickel atoms so tightly together that they cannot melt into liquid.

🧲 How Earth's Magnetic Field is Generated — Step by Step

Step 1: The outer core is made of liquid iron and nickel that are constantly moving.
Step 2: Earth's rotation causes the liquid metal to swirl in complex patterns (called convection currents).
Step 3: Moving liquid metal generates electric currents.
Step 4: These electric currents create a magnetic field around the Earth — like a giant bar magnet.
Step 5: This magnetic field extends into space and shields Earth from harmful solar radiation and cosmic rays.

Layer Interactions

How These Layers Interact 🌋

The interaction between Earth's layers causes some of the most dramatic geological events we see on our planet's surface.

🏔️

Tectonic Plate Movements

The mantle's slow movement pushes plates in the crust, causing earthquakes and the formation of mountains.

🌋

Volcanoes

Magma from the mantle rises through cracks in the crust to form volcanoes. When it erupts, we call it lava.

🧲

Magnetic Field

The movement of the liquid outer core generates Earth's magnetic field, protecting us from harmful solar radiation.

🤔 Critical Thinking

What would happen if the Earth's mantle stopped moving? How would it affect life on the surface?

  • Tectonic plates would stop moving — no more new mountains would form.
  • Volcanic activity would eventually stop — fewer nutrients recycled into the soil.
  • Earthquakes might still occur from existing plate stress but would decrease over time.
  • Without mantle convection, Earth's heat would not escape efficiently — temperature balance would be disrupted.
  • The magnetic field (driven partly by mantle-core interactions) could weaken over time.

📝 Answer the Following Questions

Q1: Name the four layers of the Earth.
The four main layers of the Earth are: 1) The Crust — the outermost, thinnest layer; 2) The Mantle — the thickest layer below the crust; 3) The Outer Core — liquid iron and nickel; 4) The Inner Core — solid ball of iron and nickel at the very centre.
Q2: Describe two characteristics each of "Crust" and "Mantle".
Crust: (1) It is the outermost and thinnest layer of Earth. (2) It is made of solid rock and soil — continental crust is mainly granite while oceanic crust is mainly basalt.

Mantle: (1) It is the thickest layer, extending about 2,900 km deep. (2) It is made of semi-solid, hot rocks that move slowly due to convection currents, causing tectonic plate movement.
Q3: Why is the inner core solid despite being the hottest layer?
The inner core is solid because of the immense pressure exerted by all the layers above it. Even though the temperature reaches 5,000–6,000°C (as hot as the Sun's surface), the extreme pressure forces iron and nickel atoms so tightly together that they cannot become liquid and remain in a solid state.
Q4: How do movements in the mantle affect the Earth's surface?
Convection currents in the mantle slowly move hot rock in circular patterns. This movement pushes the tectonic plates in the crust, causing: earthquakes (when plates suddenly slip past each other), mountain formation (when plates collide and push rock upward), and volcanic eruptions (when magma rises through cracks in the crust).
Q5: What is the difference between oceanic crust and continental crust?
Continental Crust: Made mostly of granite; forms continents; thicker (~30 km); less dense; can be very old.

Oceanic Crust: Made mostly of basalt; forms the ocean floor; thinner (5–10 km); denser; relatively younger. The oceanic crust sinks under continental crust when they collide because it is denser — a process called subduction.

🎨 Make Your Own Earth Layers Model

Materials: Playdough or modeling clay (4 different colours), a plastic knife, and labels.

  1. Choose one colour for each layer: inner core, outer core, mantle, and crust.
  2. Make a small solid ball for the inner core (hottest, solid centre).
  3. Wrap a layer around it for the outer core (liquid, slightly thicker).
  4. Add the thickest layer for the mantle — make it the largest layer!
  5. Add a thin outer layer for the crust — it should be the thinnest.
  6. Use the plastic knife to cut the model in half to show a cross-section.
  7. Label each layer with a small card or toothpick flag.

✅ Outcome: A tangible 3D model showing all four layers of Earth!

🌍 Layer Summary

graph LR Crust --> Mantle --> Core

From surface to centre, each layer becomes hotter and is under greater pressure. The journey from crust to inner core spans about 6,371 km — Earth's radius!

🔑 Key Vocabulary

  • 📖Mantle: The layer below the crust, made of hot semi-solid rock
  • 📖Magma: Molten rock inside the Earth
  • 📖Tectonic Plates: Giant pieces of crust that move slowly
  • 📖Convection: Circular movement of heated fluid or rock
  • 📖Basalt: Dark volcanic rock; makes up oceanic crust
  • 📖Granite: Light-coloured rock; makes up continental crust
