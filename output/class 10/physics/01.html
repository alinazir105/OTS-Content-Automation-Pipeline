// 02 — THEORY NOTES

Core Concepts

Detailed theory extracted directly from the syllabus content.

🌊 What Are Waves? FOUNDATION

Waves are a fundamental concept in physics that describe the transfer of energy and information through a medium or space. Crucially, waves transfer energy — not matter — from one place to another.

Waves are formed when a disturbance or energy input is applied to a medium. This disturbance can be caused by various factors, such as the motion of an object, changes in pressure, or vibrations. Once the initial disturbance occurs, it creates a ripple effect, where neighbouring particles of the medium interact with each other, transmitting the disturbance outward in all directions.

✏️ EXAMINER'S TIP

A common exam mistake is to say waves transfer matter. Always state: "Waves transfer energy, not matter." This is worth a mark in 2–3 mark questions.

💧 Water Wave in a Ripple Tank EXPERIMENT

A ripple tank is a shallow container filled with water used to study wave properties. An overhead light source shines down onto the water surface. The resulting light and dark regions on a screen or the tank's bottom allow observation and study of wave properties like wavelength, frequency, amplitude, and wave speed.

The ripple tank is a valuable tool for understanding wave behaviour in a controlled setting. Key observations include the formation of circular wavefronts from a point source and straight wavefronts from a vibrating bar.

↕️ Transverse Waves KEY CONCEPT

Transverse waves are a type of wave in which the particles of the medium move perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction in which the wave is propagating.

Regions of Transverse Waves

1. Amplitude

The maximum displacement or distance that a particle in the medium moves from its equilibrium (rest) position.

2. Crest

The highest point or peak of a transverse wave — maximum positive displacement from equilibrium.

3. Trough

The lowest point of a transverse wave — maximum displacement in the downward direction from equilibrium.

✏️ EXAMINER'S TIP

In transverse waves, particle motion is perpendicular to wave motion. Examples: light, water waves, waves on a rope. Sound is NOT a transverse wave.

↔️ Longitudinal Waves KEY CONCEPT

Longitudinal waves are a type of wave in which the particles of the medium move parallel to the direction in which the wave is propagating.

1. Compression

A region where particles are close together, resulting in a high-pressure area. Corresponds to regions of maximum particle density.

2. Rarefaction

A region where particles are spread out, resulting in a low-pressure area. Corresponds to regions of minimum particle density.

⚡ Electromagnetic vs Mechanical Waves COMPARISON

Property Electromagnetic Waves Mechanical Waves
Nature Transverse Oscillating Fields Longitudinal / Transverse
Medium Requirement No medium needed Require a medium
Propagation Can travel through vacuum Need a physical medium
Speed Speed of light in vacuum (3×10⁸ m/s) Speed depends on medium
Interaction Interact with charged particles Interact with medium
Polarization Can be polarized Polarization possible (transverse only)

✏️ EXAMINER'S TIP

Sound cannot travel through a vacuum — it is a mechanical wave. Light can travel through a vacuum — it is an electromagnetic wave. This distinction is very frequently tested!

🔁 Properties of Waves CORE

Wave properties describe behaviour when waves encounter boundaries or changes in medium.

🪞 Reflection

The bouncing back of a wave when it encounters a boundary or obstacle. Follows the law: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.

🔀 Refraction

The bending of a wave as it passes from one medium to another with different propagation speeds. The direction changes at the boundary.

🌀 Diffraction

The bending and spreading out of a wave as it encounters an obstacle or passes through an opening (aperture). Significant when obstacle size ≤ wavelength.

📏 Characteristics of Waves FORMULAE

Wavelength (λ)

Distance between two successive points in phase. For transverse: crest to crest. For longitudinal: compression to compression.

Frequency (f)

Number of complete oscillations per second. Unit: hertz (Hz).

Time Period (T)

Time for one complete oscillation. T = 1/f. Unit: seconds (s).

Wave Speed (v)

Rate at which the wave propagates through the medium. v = f × λ

Amplitude

Maximum displacement from the equilibrium position. Related to the energy carried by the wave.

Wave Front

An imaginary surface that connects all points of a wave that are in the same phase.

v = f × λ Wave speed = frequency × wavelength | T = 1/f

🔄 Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) ADVANCED

A motion repeating itself in an equal time interval is referred to as periodic or oscillatory motion.

When an object oscillates about a fixed position (mean position) and its acceleration is directly proportional to its displacement from the mean position, and is always directed towards the mean position, the motion is called Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).

a ∝ −x   →   a = −kx Acceleration is proportional to displacement, directed toward mean position. k = spring constant.

Many phenomena exhibit SHM including: electromagnetic waves, alternating current circuits, musical instruments, bridges, and molecular motion.

🕰️ Simple Pendulum & Proof of SHM PROOF

A simple pendulum is a point mass suspended by a weightless, inextensible string from a rigid support that swings back and forth under the influence of gravity.

Forces on a Displaced Pendulum

When the bob is displaced by a small angle θ, two forces act:

  • Tension T — along the string/rod
  • Weight W = mg — acting vertically downward, resolved into:
    mgsinθ (restoring force) and mgcosθ (along string)

The restoring force −mgsinθ is proportional to displacement (for small θ, sinθ ≈ θ). Thus acceleration ∝ −displacement → SHM is proved.

Energy exchange: PE is maximum at extremes (A, B); KE is maximum at mean position (O).

T = 2π √(L / g) T = period (s) | L = length of pendulum (m) | g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
✏️ EXAMINER'S TIP

The period of a simple pendulum depends ONLY on its length (L) and g — it does NOT depend on the mass of the bob or the amplitude (for small angles). This is a classic exam trap!

📉 Damped System & Damped Oscillation EXTENSION

A damped system is a mechanical system that experiences a dissipation of energy, causing the amplitude of its oscillations to decrease over time. This energy is usually lost to friction or air resistance.

In a ball and bowl system, a ball placed in a bowl-shaped hollow moves back and forth inside when displaced. The ball experiences a restoring force directed toward the centre, proportional to its displacement — this is SHM. Over time, friction causes damping and the oscillations die out.

// 04 — WORKED EXAM EXAMPLES

Solved Problems

Step-by-step solutions to typical board exam questions.

1

Calculate wave speed given frequency and wavelength

Question: A wave has a frequency of 500 Hz and a wavelength of 0.68 m. Calculate the wave speed.

  • 1
    Identify the formula

    Wave speed formula:

    v = f × λ
  • 2
    List given values

    Frequency f = 500 Hz, Wavelength λ = 0.68 m

  • 3
    Substitute and calculatev = 500 × 0.68 = 340 m/s
  • 4
    State the answer

    Wave speed v = 340 m/s (which is the speed of sound in air at room temperature — a good sanity check!)

2

Calculate the period of a simple pendulum

Question: A simple pendulum has a length of 0.25 m. Calculate its time period. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

  • 1
    Identify the formulaT = 2π √(L/g)
  • 2
    Substitute valuesT = 2π √(0.25 / 9.8)
  • 3
    Calculate step by stepL/g = 0.25/9.8 = 0.02551 → √0.02551 = 0.1597
  • 4
    Final calculationT = 2 × 3.1416 × 0.1597 ≈ 1.003 s
  • 5
    State the answer

    T ≈ 1.0 s. Note: The period does NOT depend on the mass of the bob.

3

Find the frequency and wavelength from wave speed and period

Question: A wave travels at 330 m/s with a time period of 0.002 s. Find: (a) the frequency, (b) the wavelength.

  • 1
    Part (a) — Find frequencyf = 1/T = 1/0.002 = 500 Hz
  • 2
    Part (b) — Find wavelength

    Rearrange v = f × λ → λ = v/f

    λ = 330/500 = 0.66 m
  • 3
    Final answers

    Frequency = 500 Hz, Wavelength = 0.66 m

4

Prove a pendulum's motion is SHM (short answer)

Question: Explain why the motion of a simple pendulum is Simple Harmonic Motion.

  • 1
    State definition of SHM

    SHM requires: acceleration is directly proportional to displacement from mean position and always directed toward the mean position: a = −kx

  • 2
    Identify restoring force

    For a displaced pendulum, the component of weight along the arc is the restoring force: F = −mg sin θ. For small angles, sin θ ≈ θ, so F ∝ −θ ∝ −x.

  • 3
    Link to SHM condition

    Since F ∝ −x, then by Newton's 2nd law: ma ∝ −x, so a ∝ −x. This satisfies the condition for SHM. ✓

  • 4
    Conclusion

    The motion of a simple pendulum (for small angles) satisfies the SHM condition and therefore executes Simple Harmonic Motion.
