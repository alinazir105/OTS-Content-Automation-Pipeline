3.1 Waves and Their Formation

Waves are a fundamental concept in physics that describe the transfer of energy and information through a medium or space — without the permanent transfer of matter.

Waves are formed when a disturbance or energy input is applied to a medium. This disturbance can be caused by the motion of an object, changes in pressure, or vibrations. Once the initial disturbance occurs, it creates a ripple effect — neighbouring particles interact, transmitting the disturbance outward in all directions.

💡 Key Insight: Waves transfer energy, not matter. A water wave doesn't carry water from one place to another — it carries energy.

Water Wave in a Ripple Tank: A ripple tank is a shallow container with water and an overhead light source. The resulting light and dark regions on a screen allow observation of wave properties like wavelength, frequency, amplitude, and wave speed.

🎯
Examiner's Tip

Board exams often ask what is observed in a ripple tank and how it demonstrates wave properties. Always mention the light source, water surface, and the resulting bright/dark patterns on the screen below.

↕️
3.2 Transverse Waves

In transverse waves, the particles of the medium move perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave propagation.

📈

Amplitude

Maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium (rest) position. Measured in metres (m).

⛰️

Crest

The highest point or peak of a transverse wave — maximum positive displacement from equilibrium.

🏔️

Trough

The lowest point of a transverse wave — maximum negative displacement from equilibrium position.

📐

Wavelength (λ)

Distance between two consecutive crests or troughs — one complete wave cycle in space.

🎯
Examiner's Tip

Remember: light waves and radio waves are transverse. Sound waves are longitudinal. A common trick question asks to identify wave type from a diagram — look at particle direction vs wave direction.

↔️
3.3 Longitudinal Waves

In longitudinal waves, the particles of the medium move parallel to the direction in which the wave is propagating.

🔴

Compression

A region where particles are close together — high pressure area, maximum particle density.

🔵

Rarefaction

A region where particles are spread out — low pressure area, minimum particle density.

🔊 Example: Sound waves are longitudinal waves. When you speak, your vocal cords create compressions and rarefactions in the air.
⚡

🎯
Examiner's Tip

A very common board question: "Can sound travel through vacuum?" — Answer: No! Sound is a mechanical wave requiring a medium. Light (EM wave) can travel through vacuum.

🔁
3.5 Properties of Waves
🪞

Reflection

Bouncing back of a wave when it encounters a boundary or obstacle. Follows the law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection (θᵢ = θᵣ).

🌊

Refraction

Bending of a wave as it passes from one medium to another with different propagation speeds. Degree of bending depends on the change in wave speed.

🌀

Diffraction

Bending and spreading of a wave as it encounters an obstacle or passes through an aperture. Significant when obstacle size ≤ wavelength.

🎯
Examiner's Tip

Diffraction is greatest when the gap/obstacle size is equal to or smaller than the wavelength. Sound diffracts around corners because its wavelength is large (centimetres to metres).

📏
3.6 Characteristics of Waves
λ

Wavelength (λ)

Distance between two successive in-phase points. For transverse: crest to crest. For longitudinal: compression to compression. Measured in metres.

Hz

Frequency (f)

Number of complete oscillations per second. Measured in Hertz (Hz). 1 Hz = 1 cycle per second.

⏱️

Time Period (T)

Time for one complete oscillation. T = 1/f. Measured in seconds (s).

v

Wave Speed (v)

Rate at which wave propagates. v = f × λ. Measured in m/s.

〰️

Wave Front

Imaginary surface connecting all points of a wave that are in the same phase.

v = f × λ Wave Speed = Frequency × Wavelength
T = 1 / f Time Period = 1 / Frequency

3.7 Periodic Motion and Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM)

Periodic (Oscillatory) Motion: A motion repeating itself in equal time intervals.

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): When an object oscillates about a fixed mean position such that its acceleration is:

  • Directly proportional to its displacement from the mean position
  • Always directed towards the mean position
a ∝ -x    →    a = -k × x k = spring constant, x = displacement from mean position. Negative sign indicates opposite direction.
📌 Examples of SHM: Electromagnetic waves, alternating current circuits, musical instruments, bridges, molecular motion, simple pendulum, ball in bowl.
🎯
Examiner's Tip

Key feature of SHM: acceleration is NOT constant — it varies with displacement. At equilibrium (x=0), acceleration = 0 and velocity is maximum. At extreme position, velocity = 0 and acceleration is maximum.

🕰️
3.8 Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a point mass (bob) suspended by a weightless, inextensible string from a rigid support that swings back and forth under gravity.

Forces on a Displaced Pendulum

When the bob is displaced at small angle θ:

  1. Tension (T) — along the string/rod
  2. Weight (W = mg) — vertically downward, resolved into:
    • mgsinθ (restoring force)
    • mgcosθ (balanced by tension)

Energy in a Pendulum

  • At extreme positions A and B: PE is maximum, KE = 0
  • At mean position O: KE is maximum, PE = minimum
  • Acceleration is proportional to displacement but opposite — hence SHM
T = 2π √(L / g) T = Time Period (s) | L = Length of pendulum (m) | g = 9.8 m/s² (acceleration due to gravity)
🎯
Examiner's Tip

The time period of a simple pendulum depends ONLY on its length and g — NOT on the mass of the bob or the amplitude (for small angles). This is a very frequently tested fact!

🎳
3.9 Ball & Bowl System and Damped Oscillations

Ball and Bowl System: A ball placed in a bowl-shaped hollow moves back and forth inside when displaced. This motion is SHM — the ball experiences a restoring force pulling it back to the centre, proportional to its displacement, creating repetitive back-and-forth motion.

🎳

Ball & Bowl (SHM)

Restoring force is proportional to displacement. Inertia causes the ball to overshoot the centre and oscillate — a perfect example of SHM.

📉

Damped System

A mechanical system that experiences energy dissipation, causing the amplitude of oscillations to decrease over time until motion stops.

〰️

Damped Oscillation

Oscillation where amplitude gradually decreases due to energy loss (friction, air resistance). The frequency remains nearly constant but amplitude decays.

SECTION 05

Worked Exam Examples

Step-by-step solutions to typical board examination questions.

1
Calculating Wave Speed
A wave has a frequency of 50 Hz and a wavelength of 4 m. Calculate its wave speed.
1
Identify given values:
Frequency: f = 50 Hz
Wavelength: λ = 4 m
2
Write the formula:
v = f × λ
3
Substitute values:
v = 50 × 4
4
✅ v = 200 m/s
2
Time Period of a Simple Pendulum
A simple pendulum has a length of 2.45 m. Calculate its time period. (g = 9.8 m/s²)
1
Given:
L = 2.45 m, g = 9.8 m/s²
2
Formula:
T = 2π √(L/g)
3
Calculate L/g:
L/g = 2.45 / 9.8 = 0.25
4
Take square root:
√0.25 = 0.5
5
Multiply by 2π:
T = 2 × 3.14159 × 0.5 = 3.14 s
✅ T = 3.14 seconds (approximately π seconds)
3
Frequency and Time Period Relationship
A wave completes 250 oscillations in 5 seconds. Find (a) the frequency and (b) the time period.
1
Frequency = oscillations / time:
f = 250 / 5 = 50 Hz
2
Time Period:
T = 1/f = 1/50 = 0.02 s
✅ f = 50 Hz  |  T = 0.02 s
4
Wavelength from Wave Speed
Sound travels at 340 m/s in air. If its frequency is 170 Hz, find the wavelength.
1
Given:
v = 340 m/s, f = 170 Hz
2
Rearrange v = fλ:
λ = v / f
3
λ = 340 / 170 = 2 m
✅ λ = 2 metres
