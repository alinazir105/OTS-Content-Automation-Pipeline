Core Theory

🔊 2.1 Sound Waves

Sound waves are vibrations of air particles that travel through a medium, such as air, and can be detected by our ears. These waves carry sound energy and enable us to hear sounds in our surroundings.

Sound waves are longitudinal waves, which means that the particles in the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation — unlike transverse waves where particles vibrate perpendicularly.

Compressions

Regions where air particles are densely packed together, leading to higher air pressure.

Rarefactions

Regions where air particles are spread apart, resulting in lower air pressure compared to surrounding areas.

💡
Examiner's Tip

Always state that sound is a longitudinal wave. In exam answers, mention that particles vibrate parallel to the direction of propagation. Diagrams should clearly show regions of compression and rarefaction.

🎵 2.2 Production of Sound by Vibrating Sources

When an object vibrates, it causes the air particles around it to vibrate as well. These vibrations create pressure variations in the air, which then propagate as sound waves.

Frequency → Pitch

The frequency of the vibrations determines the pitch of the sound. Higher frequency = higher pitch.

Amplitude → Loudness

The amplitude of the vibrations affects the loudness of the sound. Greater amplitude = louder sound.

🔬 2.3 Electric Bell Jar Experiment

The electric bell jar experiment demonstrates that sound cannot travel through a vacuum, as sound requires a medium to propagate, such as air, water, or solids.

  1. Set up an electric bell and a vacuum bell jar apparatus. The electric bell consists of a vibrating clapper that strikes the bell when electric current passes through it.

  2. Connect the electric bell to a power source and ensure it is working correctly, producing audible sound in the surrounding air.

  3. Place the electric bell apparatus inside the vacuum bell jar and seal it airtight.

  4. Gradually pump out the air from the vacuum bell jar using a vacuum pump until a near-vacuum condition is achieved.

  5. Observe that as the air is removed from the jar and the pressure decreases, the sound produced by the electric bell becomes fainter and eventually cannot be heard at all.

  6. This confirms that sound requires a medium, such as air, to travel. In the absence of air (vacuum), the sound waves have no particles to propagate through, resulting in silence.

💡
Examiner's Tip

The bell jar experiment is a common exam question. Key conclusion: "Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because it is a mechanical wave requiring a medium." Note that you can still see the bell clapper move — it's just the sound that disappears!

⚡ 2.4 Speed of Sound

The speed of sound varies depending on the medium through which it travels. It is generally fastest in solids, followed by liquids, and slowest in gases.

Method 1: Measuring Sound Between Two Points

An observer measures the time taken for sound to travel between two points at a known distance apart. By dividing the distance by the time taken, the speed of sound can be calculated.

v = d / t
Speed of Sound (Direct Method)

Method 2: Using Echoes

A loud sound is produced and the time taken for the echo to return after reflecting off a distant surface is measured. The distance to the surface can then be calculated.

d = (v/2) × t
Distance to Reflecting Surface (Echo Method)
d = distance (m) v = speed of sound (m/s) t = total time for echo (s)

Speed in Solids

In solids, particles are closely packed with strong intermolecular forces, leading to high elasticity. Speed of sound is highest in solids.

vs = √(E / ρ)
Speed of Sound in Solids
vs = speed in solid (m/s) E = Young's modulus (Pa) ρ = density (kg/m³)

Speed in Liquids

vl = √(K / ρ)
Speed of Sound in Liquids
vl = speed in liquid (m/s) K = Bulk modulus (Pa) ρ = density (kg/m³)

Speed in Gases

vg = √(γP / ρ)
Speed of Sound in Gases
vg = speed in gas (m/s) γ = adiabatic index (Cp/Cv) P = pressure (Pa) ρ = density (kg/m³)

📊 2.5 Factors Affecting Speed of Sound

1. Elasticity

Speed is directly proportional to elasticity. More elastic media (solids) allow sound to travel faster.

2. Density

Speed is inversely proportional to density. Denser materials allow sound to propagate faster due to stronger forces.

3. Temperature

Speed increases with temperature. Warmer temperatures cause particles to vibrate more rapidly, facilitating faster transmission.

Effect of Temperature — Ideal Gas Formula

vg = √(γRT / M)
Speed of Sound vs Temperature (Ideal Gas)
R = Gas constant T = Temperature (Kelvin) M = Molar mass of gas

This shows that speed is directly proportional to the square root of temperature. As temperature increases, molecular kinetic energy increases, resulting in higher sound speed.

Speed of Sound in Air at Temperature T°C

va = 331.5 × √(1 + T/273.15)
Speed in Air (m/s) where T is in °C
331.5 m/s = speed at 0°C T = temperature in °C

Effect of Humidity

Higher humidity levels lead to a decrease in the speed of sound in air due to the presence of water vapor molecules, which influence the air's density and compressibility.

💡
Examiner's Tip

Students often confuse humidity's effect: humid air is actually less dense than dry air (water vapour is lighter than N₂/O₂), so sound travels slightly faster in humid air. However, this formula reflects standard conditions. Always check your board's expected answer.

👁️ 2.6 Characteristics of Sound (Seeing Sounds)

1. Quality (Timbre)

The timbre or tonal color of a sound. Allows us to distinguish between different sources — e.g., a violin vs a piano playing the same note.

2. Loudness

Corresponds to the amplitude or intensity of a sound wave. Determines how soft or loud a sound appears to our ears.

3. Pitch

Related to the frequency of a sound wave. Defines how high or low a sound is perceived. Higher frequency = higher pitch.

4. Sound Intensity

Measures the amount of sound energy that passes through a unit area. Affects how "strong" a sound is perceived by our ears.

🔁 2.8 Reflection of Sound (Echo)

An echo occurs when sound waves encounter a surface and bounce back towards the source. The time delay depends on:

  • The distance between the sound source and the reflecting surface
  • The speed of sound in the medium
  • The time taken for the sound to travel to the surface and back
d = (v × t) / 2     v = 2d / t
Echo Formulae
d = distance to surface (m) v = speed of sound (m/s) t = total time for echo (s)
💡
Examiner's Tip

Always divide by 2 in echo problems! The sound travels to the surface AND back. The most common mistake is forgetting this and using the full travel time.

🦇 2.9 Ultrasound, Infrasound & Audible Range

Infrasonic

Below 20 Hz. Humans cannot hear. Used by elephants and whales for communication.

Audible Range

20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The range detectable by the human ear.

Ultrasound

Above 20,000 Hz. Used in medicine, industry, and SONAR navigation.

Audible Frequency Ranges by Species

Applications of Ultrasound

1. Cleansing

Generates tiny bubbles that collapse and create pressure waves, removing contaminants without damage.

2. Quality Control

Detects defects or flaws in materials by analyzing sound wave responses through materials.

3. SONAR

Sound Navigation and Ranging — used for underwater navigation, mapping, and locating submarines.

4. Echocardiography

Creates real-time images of the heart's structure and function for cardiac diagnosis.

5. Ultrasonography

Non-invasive imaging using high-frequency sound waves. Used for pregnancy monitoring, abdominal scans, and vascular diseases.

💡
Examiner's Tip

Ultrasonography does NOT use ionizing radiation (unlike X-rays). This makes it safe for pregnant patients. Mention the transducer device in exam answers — it both emits and receives sound waves.

Worked Exam Examples

1
Echo Problem — Finding Distance to a Cliff
▼
Question: A person standing near a cliff claps their hands and hears the echo after 2.4 seconds. If the speed of sound in air is 340 m/s, calculate the distance from the person to the cliff.
1
Identify Given Information

Speed of sound, v = 340 m/s | Time for echo, t = 2.4 s

2
Select the Correct Formula

For echo problems: d = (v × t) / 2
We divide by 2 because the sound travels to the cliff AND back.

3
Substitute Values

d = (340 × 2.4) / 2 = 816 / 2 = 408 m

✅ Answer: d = 408 metres
2
Speed of Sound — Finding Speed from Echo Timing
▼
Question: A SONAR pulse is sent from a ship and returns after 0.6 seconds. The ocean floor is 450 m below the ship. Calculate the speed of sound in sea water.
1
Identify Given Information

Distance to ocean floor, d = 450 m | Total time, t = 0.6 s

2
Select Formula

v = 2d / t (rearranged echo formula)

3
Substitute and Calculate

v = (2 × 450) / 0.6 = 900 / 0.6 = 1500 m/s

✅ Answer: v = 1500 m/s
3
Speed of Sound in Air at Given Temperature
▼
Question: Calculate the speed of sound in air at a temperature of 25°C.
1
Formula for Speed in Air

va = 331.5 × √(1 + T/273.15)

2
Substitute T = 25°C

va = 331.5 × √(1 + 25/273.15)
va = 331.5 × √(1 + 0.09155)
va = 331.5 × √(1.09155)

3
Calculate

va = 331.5 × 1.04477
va ≈ 346.3 m/s

✅ Answer: va ≈ 346.3 m/s
4
Direct Method — Measuring Speed of Sound
▼
Question: An observer sees a lightning flash and hears thunder 3 seconds later. The lightning struck 1020 m away. Calculate the speed of sound in air.
1
Given

Distance d = 1020 m | Time t = 3 s | (Light travels almost instantly)

2
Formula

v = d / t

3
Calculate

v = 1020 / 3 = 340 m/s

✅ Answer: v = 340 m/s
5
Ultrasound — Medical Imaging Depth Calculation
▼
Question: An ultrasound pulse sent into human tissue reflects from an organ and returns in 0.00008 s. If the speed of ultrasound in tissue is 1540 m/s, find the depth of the organ.
1
Given

v = 1540 m/s | t = 0.00008 s = 8 × 10⁻⁵ s

2
Formula

d = (v × t) / 2

3
Calculate

d = (1540 × 8×10⁻⁵) / 2 = 0.1232 / 2 = 0.0616 m
d = 6.16 cm

✅ Answer: Depth = 6.16 cm
