About This Chapter

Light plays an essential role in how we see the world around us. It interacts with different surfaces and objects in fascinating ways. In this chapter, we will explore how light behaves when it reflects, how shadows are formed, and which objects allow light to pass.

Light behaves differently when it hits various surfaces or objects. Let's explore three main types of interactions and the science behind them!

CORE CONCEPT

Light & Objects

How objects interact with light depends on their material. There are three main categories:

🪟

Transparent

Allow light to pass through them completely without scattering it.

e.g., Clear Glass
🌫️

Translucent

Allow some light to pass through, but scatter it so objects look blurry.

e.g., Frosted Glass
🧱

Opaque

Do not allow any light to pass through — they absorb or reflect it.

e.g., Wood, Metal

CONCEPT

Reflection

Reflection happens when light bounces off a surface. Smooth and shiny surfaces, like mirrors, reflect light clearly. This is why we can see our face in a mirror.

Think of it like throwing a ball at a wall — the ball bounces back. Light does the same thing when it hits a smooth surface!

Law of Reflection:
Angle of Incidence = Angle of Reflection
The incoming and outgoing light rays make equal angles with the surface.

🪞 Specular Reflection

On smooth, shiny surfaces (mirrors), light reflects in one direction. This gives a clear image. Examples: mirrors, still water, polished metal.

🧱 Diffuse Reflection

On rough surfaces (walls, paper), light scatters in many directions. No clear image forms, but the surface is still visible. This is why we can see non-shiny objects!

❓

Did You Know? (Click to reveal!)

A mirror reflects about 95% of light that hits it — that's why you see such a clear reflection!

💡

Fun Science! (Click to reveal!)

Still water acts like a mirror because its smooth surface creates specular reflection — just like a glass mirror!

CONCEPT

Shadows

Shadows are formed when an opaque object blocks the path of light. Shadows always appear on the side opposite the light source. The size and shape of a shadow depend on the position of the object and the light.

WORKED EXAMPLES

Step-by-Step Examples

Example 1: Why can you see through a glass window?

1
Identify the object: a clear glass window.
2
Ask: Does light pass through? → Yes, all of it.
3
Classify: Clear glass is Transparent.
4
Conclusion: Because light passes completely through, we can see clearly through it. No shadow is formed.
Transparent objects let ALL light through. Think of glass as having no "barriers" for light rays.

Example 2: Why does a tree create a shadow on a sunny day?

1
Identify the object: a tree (wood, leaves).
2
Does light pass through? → No. The tree is opaque.
3
The tree blocks the Sun's light on one side.
4
A shadow forms on the opposite side of the tree from the Sun.
5
If it's noon (Sun directly above), shadow is small. If it's morning/evening (Sun at an angle), shadow is long.
Shadows always appear on the OPPOSITE side from the light source. Think about where the Sun is and where the darkness would fall.

Example 3: How does moving a flashlight change a shadow's size?

1
Place a book (opaque object) between a flashlight and a wall.
2
Move the flashlight very close to the book → Shadow is LARGE on the wall.
3
Move the flashlight far away from the book → Shadow becomes SMALL on the wall.
4
Rule: Closer to light = Larger shadow; Farther from light = Smaller shadow.
Imagine the light spreading out like a cone. When the object is close, it blocks more of the cone → bigger shadow. When far, it blocks less → smaller shadow.

CHALLENGE

Critical Thinking

🧠 Think Deeply!

Challenge 1

Why do we see our reflection clearly in a mirror but not on a wall, even though both surfaces reflect light?

A mirror has an extremely smooth, flat surface that creates specular reflection — all light bounces back in the same direction, forming a clear image. A wall is rough, causing diffuse reflection — light scatters in all directions, so no clear image is formed. The surface texture is the key difference!

Challenge 2

Why do opaque objects create clear shadows, but translucent ones do not?

Opaque objects block ALL light rays in a defined area, creating a sharp, dark shadow with clear edges. Translucent objects let some light through — scattered in many directions — so only a faint, blurry shadow forms (or none at all). The more light passes through, the weaker the shadow.

Challenge 3

What is the main difference between frosted glass and clear glass in terms of light passage?

Clear glass is transparent: light passes straight through without scattering, so you can see objects clearly. Frosted glass is translucent: the rough or textured surface scatters light in many directions, letting light in but making objects appear blurry. Both let some light through, but only clear glass lets you see distinct shapes through it.

ACTIVITY

Create Shadows of Different Objects

🔬 Materials Needed

🔦 Flashlight 🪟 Transparent Glass 🌫️ Frosted Plastic Sheet 🪵 Wooden Block

📋 Steps

1️⃣

Shine on Glass

Shine the flashlight on the transparent glass to see light passing through completely. Notice: no shadow forms!

2️⃣

Shine on Frosted Plastic

Shine the flashlight on the frosted plastic sheet to notice partial light passing through. Notice: faint/blurry shadow.

3️⃣

Shine on Wood Block

Shine the flashlight on the wooden block to observe a clear, dark shadow. The wood is opaque — it blocks all light!

4️⃣

Move the Wood Block

Move the wooden block closer to the flashlight → shadow gets larger. Move it farther → shadow gets smaller. Observe and record your findings!

FUN FACTS

Amazing Light Facts! 💡

⏱️

How long does sunlight take to reach Earth? (Click!)

It takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds for light to travel from the Sun to Earth — that's 150 million kilometres!

📏

Does light travel in curves? (Click!)

Light always travels in a straight line! This is why shadows have sharp edges and why beams of sunlight through windows look straight.

🌈

What is white light made of? (Click!)

White light is made up of all the colours of the rainbow — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and violet (ROYGBIV)!
