What are Modal Verbs?

Before we dive into May and Might, let's understand what modal verbs are and why they matter.

Discovery Zone: Modal Verbs

Modal verbs are special helping verbs that give extra meaning to the main verb. They help us express things like possibility, permission, ability, and obligation. Common modal verbs include: cancouldmaymightwillwouldshouldmust

📌 Grammar Rule – Modal Verb Structure
Subject + Modal Verb + Base Form of Main Verb

Example: She may come tomorrow.
Example: It might rain today.
💡

Did You Know?

Click to reveal!

Modal verbs never change their form — no -s, -ed, or -ing endings. We always say "she might go", never "she mights go"!

🌟

Fun Fact

Click to reveal!

The word "might" was originally the past tense of "may" in Old English! Today, both are used to express present/future possibility.

✏️

Quick Tip

Click to reveal!

After a modal verb, always use the base form of the verb — no "to". Say "She may go", not "She may to go".

Core Concept

Understanding May and Might — their meanings, uses, and differences.

📊 Possibility Meter — May vs Might

MAY
50% chance
MIGHT
30% chance
Modal Verb Meaning Positive Example Negative Example
May Shows possibility — about 50% chance of happening We may go to the zoo tomorrow. We may not (mayn't) go to the zoo tomorrow.
Might Possible but less likely — about 30% chance It might rain today. It might not (mightn't) rain today.

When to use May vs Might?

While both words express possibility, use these guidelines:

  • MAY Use when something has a reasonable/fair chance of happening. You feel it's likely enough to be mentioned. (~50% confidence)
  • MIGHT Use when something is less certain or you're less sure it will happen. Expresses a smaller degree of possibility. (~30% confidence)
Memory Trick: "MAY = More likely" — both start with 'M-A'. When you see 'may', think "Maybe, there's a good chance!" When you see 'might', think "Hmm, I'm not so sure… it MIGHT happen."
📌 Negative Forms
may + not = may not (short form: mayn't)

might + not = might not (short form: mightn't)

Note: "mayn't" is rarely used in modern English. "might not" and "mightn't" are more common.

Worked Examples

Study these carefully crafted examples to understand how to use May and Might in sentences.

✅ MAY – Positive

We may have visitors tomorrow.

→ We may not have visitors tomorrow.
✅ MIGHT – Positive

It might snow this evening.

→ It might not snow this evening.
🌤️ MAY – Weather

I may go to the beach if the weather is nice.

→ I may not go if it gets cloudy.
🚦 MIGHT – Traffic

She might be late for the meeting because of the traffic.

→ She might not arrive on time.
🏛️ MAY – Plans

We may visit the museum next weekend.

→ We may not get the chance to go.
🏆 MIGHT – Competition

He might win the race if he trains hard enough.

→ He might not win without more practice.

Open Challenge

Answer these questions in full sentences using may or might.

🎯 Exercise 3 – Answer Using May or Might

1. Do you think it will rain tomorrow?
2. Do you think your friend will come to your birthday party?
3. Will you finish your homework before dinner?
4. Are you likely to visit the museum next week?
5. Do you think the teacher will give us extra homework?
Tips for great answers:
• Start with the subject (I, She, He, We, They, It)
• Use may or might after the subject
• Add the base form of the verb (go, come, rain, visit)
• Add a reason with "because" or "if" to make your answer complete!

Example: "It might rain tomorrow because the sky is cloudy and dark."
