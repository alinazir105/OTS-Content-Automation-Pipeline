Abstract

What is Stoichiometry?

A formal introduction to the branch of chemistry governing quantitative relationships in reactions.

Stoichiometry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relative quantities of reactants and products in chemical reactions. It involves calculations to determine the amounts of various substances involved in reactions.

The word stoichiometry derives from two Greek words: stoicheion (element) and metron (measure). It is the mathematical backbone of chemistry, allowing us to predict outcomes of reactions with precision.

At the heart of stoichiometry lies the mole concept — a counting unit that bridges the atomic world (where individual atoms and molecules operate) with the macroscopic world we can observe and measure in the laboratory.

"Chemistry is the science of matter, but I prefer to say chemistry is the science of stuff — and stoichiometry tells us exactly how much stuff we need."
— Principle of Quantitative Chemistry

Stoichiometry applies the laws of conservation of mass and the law of definite proportions, allowing chemists to scale reactions from laboratory to industrial settings. Understanding yield — theoretical, actual, and percent — is central to evaluating the efficiency of any chemical process.

02
Theory

Core Definitions & Concepts

Explore the fundamental building blocks of stoichiometry through formal definitions and structured analysis.

⚛
Atom
The smallest unit of an element that enters into a chemical reaction. An atom is the smallest particle of an element that takes part in chemical change. It cannot be further divided by ordinary chemical means.
🧱
Element
A substance which cannot be split into two or more simpler substances by ordinary chemical means. It is a pure substance or least complex form of matter and by simple chemical reaction cannot be converted into another simpler substance.
💎
Substance
The purest form of matter. Substances contain only one type of atom or molecule. They have definite and uniform composition throughout — either elements or compounds.
#️⃣
Atomic Number (Z)
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of an element. It is equal to the number of electrons orbiting round the nucleus of that neutral atom. Denoted by "Z".
Z = number of protons = number of electrons (neutral atom)
🔢
Mass Number (A)
Also called Nucleon Number. The sum of protons and neutrons present in the nucleus of an atom. Represents the total number of nucleons.
A = P + n  OR  A = Z + n
⚖
Atomic Mass
The atomic mass of a single atom is simply its total mass. It is typically expressed in atomic mass units (amu). One amu is defined as 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
Definition 1.1
Relative Atomic Mass (Atomic Weight)

The relative atomic mass (Ar) of an element is defined as the ratio of the average mass of an atom to 1/12th the mass of a ¹²C (carbon-12) atom.

Ar = (Average mass of one atom of the element) / (1/12 × mass of one ¹²C atom)
This ratio is dimensionless — relative atomic mass has no units
∎
Definition 2.1
The Mole

One mole is the amount of substance that contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ atoms, molecules, or ions. This number is also known as Avogadro's Number, named after Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro.

The mole is the SI unit for amount of substance. It bridges the microscopic world of atoms with the macroscopic world of laboratory measurements.

NA = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
Avogadro's Constant — the number of entities in one mole
∎
Definition 2.2
Avogadro's Number

The Avogadro constant is defined as the number of atoms in 12 grams of the C-12 isotope. One mole of a substance contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles — this number is called Avogadro's number.

Avogadro's number was proposed by French scientist Jean Perrin to recognise Avogadro's work. The word "mole" derives from molecule.

∎
Definition 2.3
Molar Volume

The molar volume is the volume occupied by one mole of any gas at a given temperature and pressure. At Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP), this is approximately:

Vm = 22.4 L mol⁻¹ (at STP)
Valid for ideal gases; standard conditions: 0°C (273 K), 1 atm
∎

Analogy — Coins and Moles

Using moles is analogous to weighing coin rolls to estimate number of coins. The value of coins = mass (different for different coins), the number of coins = number of atoms, and the rolls of coins = moles of atoms.

🧪
Molecular Mass
The sum of all atomic masses present in a molecule. It represents the total mass of one molecule of a compound, calculated by adding together the atomic masses of each atom in the molecular formula, accounting for the number of each type of atom.
Molecular mass = Σ (atomic mass × number of atoms)
🔩
Formula Mass
The sum of all atomic masses present in a formula unit of an ionic compound. Used for ionic compounds (like NaCl) where discrete molecules do not exist — instead we refer to formula units. It is calculated the same way as molecular mass.
Formula mass = Σ (atomic mass × subscript for each element)
Worked Principle 3.1
Calculating Molecular Mass: H₂O

For water (H₂O):

→ Hydrogen (H): atomic mass = 1.008 amu; 2 atoms → 2 × 1.008 = 2.016

→ Oxygen (O): atomic mass = 16.00 amu; 1 atom → 1 × 16.00 = 16.00

→ Molecular mass of H₂O = 2.016 + 16.00 = 18.016 g/mol

∎
Worked Principle 3.2
Calculating Formula Mass: NaCl

For sodium chloride (NaCl):

→ Sodium (Na): atomic mass = 22.99 amu; 1 atom → 22.99

→ Chlorine (Cl): atomic mass = 35.45 amu; 1 atom → 35.45

→ Formula mass of NaCl = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol

∎
📐
Theoretical Yield
The maximum amount of product that can be generated as predicted by the chemical equation, assuming every molecule of reactant converts into product. It is based purely on stoichiometry, without accounting for practical limitations.
Based on the balanced equation & stoichiometric ratios
🧫
Actual Yield
What is actually obtained from the reaction after it has been carried out and products isolated. Almost invariably lower than theoretical yield due to incomplete reactions, by-products, side reactions, and losses during purification.
Measured experimentally after reaction completion
📊
Percent Yield
A measure of the efficiency of a reaction. Calculated by dividing the actual yield by the theoretical yield, then multiplying by 100. It provides insight into losses occurring in practical settings and is vital for industrial process optimization.
% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100
Theorem 4.1
Limiting Reactant

The limiting reactant is the reactant which is present in the least (stoichiometric) amount, or the one that is entirely consumed first in a chemical reaction. Once the limiting reactant is consumed, the reaction stops, regardless of how much of the other reactant(s) remain.

Analogy: Start with six slices of bread and two slices of cheese. The sandwich equation is: 2 bread + 1 cheese → 1 sandwich. Even though you have extra bread, cheese (limiting reactant) limits production to only 2 sandwiches.

The excess reactant is the one that remains after the limiting reactant is consumed.

Theoretical yield is always calculated from the limiting reactant
∎
Why is Practical Yield Less Than Theoretical Yield?

Some reactions are reversible (equilibrium prevents complete conversion). Side reactions produce different products. Practical considerations like purity of reactants, temperature control, measurement precision, and loss of product during extraction or transfer all reduce actual yield.

Definition 5.1
Scientific (Exponential) Notation

Scientific notation (also called exponential notation) is a way of expressing numbers that are too large or too small to be conveniently written in decimal form. It is composed of a coefficient and a power of 10.

The coefficient is always a number between 1 and 10 (inclusive of 1, exclusive of 10). The exponent indicates how many places the decimal point has been moved.

Number = a × 10n   where 1 ≤ a < 10
a = coefficient, n = integer exponent (positive or negative)
∎

Derivations & Proof

Step-by-Step Reasoning

Formal derivations of core stoichiometric relationships from first principles.

Derivation 3.1
The Mole-Mass-Number Triangle

Starting from the definition of Avogadro's number and molar mass, we can derive all fundamental conversion relationships:

1

Given: Avogadro's Number

By definition, one mole contains NA = 6.022 × 10²³ entities (atoms, molecules, or ions).

NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹
2

Define Molar Mass (M)

The molar mass M is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol). Numerically equal to the atomic/molecular mass in amu.

M = mass of 1 mole (g mol⁻¹)
3

Derive Moles from Mass

If we have m grams of a substance with molar mass M:

n = m / M   (moles = mass ÷ molar mass)
4

Number of Particles from Moles

The number of particles N in n moles:

N = n × NA = (m/M) × 6.022 × 10²³
5

Volume for Gases at STP

For ideal gases at STP, using molar volume Vm = 22.4 L/mol:

V = n × Vm = (m/M) × 22.4 L
Complete Conversion Framework
Summary: All Mole Conversions

The three fundamental conversion pathways:

Mass (g) ⟷ Moles (mol) ⟷ Particles ⟷ Volume (L at STP)
n = m/M  |  N = n·Nₐ  |  V = n × 22.4
∎

Worked Examples

Step-by-Step Solutions

Comprehensive worked examples applying all stoichiometric concepts from first principles.

Example 1 Mole Calculation — Converting Mass to Moles

Problem: How many moles are present in 36 grams of water (H₂O)?

Step 1: Find Molar Mass of H₂O

M(H₂O) = 2 × M(H) + 1 × M(O) = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.016 g/mol

Step 2: Apply Mole Formula

n = m / M = 36 g ÷ 18.016 g/mol

Step 3: Calculate

n = 36 / 18.016 ≈ 2.0 moles of H₂O

Step 4: Number of Molecules

N = n × NA = 2.0 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

Example 2 Theoretical Yield Calculation — Synthesis of Water

Problem: If 4 grams of H₂ reacts with excess O₂, what is the theoretical yield of water?

Step 1: Write the Balanced Equation

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 2: Convert H₂ Mass to Moles

M(H₂) = 2 × 1.008 = 2.016 g/mol

n(H₂) = 4 g ÷ 2.016 g/mol = 1.984 mol ≈ 2.0 mol

Step 3: Apply Stoichiometric Ratio

From equation: 2 mol H₂ → 2 mol H₂O (1:1 ratio)

n(H₂O) = 2.0 mol

Step 4: Convert Moles to Mass

M(H₂O) = 18.016 g/mol

mass(H₂O) = 2.0 mol × 18.016 g/mol = 36.0 g (Theoretical Yield)

Example 3 Percent Yield Calculation

Problem: In the reaction above, if only 30.0 g of water was actually collected, what is the percent yield?

Step 1: Identify Yields

Theoretical yield = 36.0 g (from Example 2)
Actual yield = 30.0 g (measured experimentally)

Step 2: Apply Percent Yield Formula

% Yield = (Actual Yield / Theoretical Yield) × 100

Step 3: Calculate

% Yield = (30.0 / 36.0) × 100 = 83.3%

Interpretation

The 16.7% loss could be due to water evaporating, incomplete reaction, side reactions, or loss during collection. This is a typical yield for many laboratory reactions.

Example 4 Limiting Reactant Identification

Problem: 10 g of H₂ reacts with 72 g of O₂. Identify the limiting reactant. Equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert Both Reactants to Moles

n(H₂) = 10 g ÷ 2.016 g/mol = 4.96 mol
n(O₂) = 72 g ÷ 32.00 g/mol = 2.25 mol

Step 2: Determine Stoichiometric Requirements

From equation: 2 mol H₂ requires 1 mol O₂ (ratio 2:1)
4.96 mol H₂ requires 4.96/2 = 2.48 mol O₂

Step 3: Compare Available vs Required

O₂ available = 2.25 mol; O₂ required for all H₂ = 2.48 mol
O₂ is insufficient → O₂ is the limiting reactant

Step 4: Calculate Theoretical Yield Using Limiting Reactant

n(H₂O) = 2 × n(O₂) = 2 × 2.25 = 4.50 mol
mass(H₂O) = 4.50 × 18.016 = 81.07 g

Example 5 Avogadro's Number Application

Problem: How many atoms are in 63.5 g of copper (Cu)? (MCu = 63.5 g/mol)

Step 1: Find Moles of Copper

n(Cu) = 63.5 g ÷ 63.5 g/mol = 1.00 mol

Step 2: Apply Avogadro's Number

N = n × NA = 1.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms/mol

Step 3: Result

N = 6.022 × 10²³ copper atoms

This is exactly one mole — which makes sense since 63.5 g is the molar mass of copper!

07
Advanced Applications

Industry & Beyond

How stoichiometry shapes the modern world — from industrial chemistry to pharmaceutical synthesis.

🏭 Industrial Manufacturing

In the Haber-Bosch process (production of ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃), stoichiometry determines the precise ratio of nitrogen to hydrogen needed. At industrial scale, even 1% improvement in yield represents millions of dollars of savings and reduced raw material consumption.

Key Application

Fertiliser production: ~1.8 × 10⁸ tonnes/year of ammonia, stoichiometrically balanced to minimise waste

💊 Pharmaceutical Synthesis

Drug manufacturing demands extremely high percent yields of pure products. Stoichiometric calculations determine the exact amounts of reactants, ensuring no excess of expensive or toxic starting materials. Multi-step syntheses compound yield losses — a 5-step synthesis each at 90% yield gives only 59% overall yield.

Critical Insight

Overall yield = Product of individual step yields: (0.90)⁵ = 0.59 = 59%

🌍 Environmental Impact

Stoichiometry is central to understanding pollution. The combustion of octane: 2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O. Each mole of octane burned produces 8 moles of CO₂. Knowing this allows calculation of exact carbon footprints and enables the design of catalytic converters with stoichiometrically correct fuel-to-oxygen ratios.

Real Calculation

1 litre of petrol ≈ 740 g ≈ 6.49 mol octane → 51.9 mol CO₂ → 2.28 kg CO₂ per litre

🔬 Isotopes & Relative Atomic Mass

Most elements exist as mixtures of isotopes. Chlorine consists of approximately 75% ³⁵Cl and 25% ³⁷Cl. The relative atomic mass is the weighted average:

Ar(Cl) = (0.75 × 35) + (0.25 × 37) = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5
This explains why the periodic table shows Cl = 35.5, not a whole number

This concept of weighted averages is fundamental to understanding why atomic masses are not whole numbers, and underlies mass spectrometry analysis used in modern chemical research.

⚗ Concept Check: X₂ + Y Reaction

Consider the hypothetical reaction of element X₂(g) with element Y(g) to produce XY(g).

Balanced Equation

X₂(g) + 2Y(g) → 2XY(g)

Interpretation

For every 1 molecule of X₂, we need 2 molecules of Y. If we start with 3 molecules X₂ and 4 molecules Y: X₂ needs 6 Y, but only 4 are available. Y is the limiting reactant.

Products formed

4 molecules Y → 4 molecules XY. Remaining: 1 molecule X₂ unreacted (excess).
