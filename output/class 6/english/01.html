Definition

What are Stative Verbs?

Stative verbs describe a state or a condition rather than an action. They tell us about being, having, or experiencing something.

1

"To Be" — States & Existence

Used to describe conditions and the existence of somebody or something. Forms: is, am, are, was, were, will be.

2

"Has / Have / Had" — Possession

Used to show illness, possession, relationships, and nature. Example: She has a car. They have an idea. I had a wonderful time.

3

"Been" — Past + Present State

Describes a past state which also exists in the present.
Past (was/were) + Present (is/am/are) = Present Perfect (been)

💡

Key Insight

Unlike action verbs (run, jump, eat), stative verbs are usually not used in continuous tenses. You say "I am happy" — not "I am being happy" (usually).

🌟 Stative Verbs — Overview

Stative verbs describe a state or condition, not an action. They tell us how things ARE, not what things DO. The three main groups are: "To Be", "Has/Have/Had", and "Been".

She is happy They have a house He has been busy

🔵 "To Be" — Describing States & Existence

The verb "to be" in its stative use describes conditions and the existence of somebody or something. It changes form based on tense and subject.

I am happy ✓ She is a doctor ✓ They were late ✓ He will be there ✓

🟢 "Has / Have / Had" — Possession & More

Used to show illness, possession, relationships, and nature. Use "has" with he/she/it and "have" with I/you/we/they. "Had" is the past form.

She has a car ✓ I have an idea ✓ He had a pet ✓ She has a fever ✓

🟣 "Been" — Past + Present State Combined

"Been" describes a past state which also exists in the present. It's the past participle of "be" and is used with has/have to form the present perfect.

Formula: Past state (was/were) + Present state (is/am/are) = Present perfect state (been)

She has been happy ✓ They have been busy ✓ He has been sick ✓

⭐ Present States

We use is/am/are to describe current states and has/have for current possession.

She is happy She has a pet They are tired

⭐ Present Perfect States

We use has/have + been or has/have + had to describe states that started in the past and continue to the present.

She has been happy She has had a pet

⭐ Past States

We use was/were for past states and had for past possession.

She was happy She had a pet They were late
Grammar

Verb Forms: Affirmative, Negative & Questions

See how each stative verb changes form across different sentence types.

1. IS / AM / ARE — Present States
✅ Affirmative
  • She is a doctor.
  • I am happy.
  • They are tired.
❌ Negative
  • She is not (isn't) a doctor.
  • I am not happy.
  • They are not (aren't) tired.
❓ Questions
  • Is she a doctor?
  • Am I happy?
  • Are they tired?
2. WAS / WERE — Past States
✅ Affirmative
  • He was at the party.
  • We were late.
❌ Negative
  • He was not (wasn't) at the party.
  • We were not (weren't) late.
❓ Questions
  • Was he at the party?
  • Were we late?
3. WILL BE — Future States
✅ Affirmative
  • She will be there soon.
  • I will be happy to help.
❌ Negative
  • She will not (won't) be there soon.
  • I will not (won't) be happy to help.
❓ Questions
  • Will she be there soon?
  • Will I be happy to help?
4. HAS / HAVE / HAD — Possession
✅ Affirmative
  • She has a car.
  • They have an idea.
  • I had a wonderful time.
❌ Negative
  • She does not (doesn't) have a car.
  • They do not (don't) have an idea.
  • I did not (didn't) have a wonderful time.
❓ Questions
  • Does she have a car?
  • Do they have an idea?
  • Did I have a wonderful time?
5. HAS BEEN / HAVE BEEN / HAD BEEN — Present & Past Perfect
✅ Affirmative
  • She has been hard-working.
  • They have been busy.
  • He has had a rabbit.
  • She had had a difficult time before the success.
❌ Negative
  • She has not (hasn't) been hard-working.
  • They have not (haven't) been busy.
  • He has not (hasn't) had a rabbit.
  • She had not (hadn't) had a difficult time before the success.
❓ Questions
  • Has she been hard-working?
  • Have they been busy?
  • Has he had a rabbit?
  • Had she had a difficult time before the success?

Advanced

Deep Reading Panels

Explore additional grammar insights for advanced learners.

Stative verbs describe states that are relatively stable — they don't show an action in progress. For this reason, they typically cannot be used in continuous tenses. For example:

  • ✅ Correct: "I know the answer." (stative)
  • ❌ Incorrect: "I am knowing the answer." (wrong)
  • ✅ Correct: "She has a car." (stative)
  • ❌ Incorrect: "She is having a car." (wrong)

However, some verbs like "have" can be used in continuous form when they describe an action (not a state): "She is having lunch." (action = eating)

The construction "had had" might look strange at first, but it has a clear purpose. The first "had" is the auxiliary verb forming the past perfect, and the second "had" is the main verb (past participle of have).

  • Example: "She had had a pet before she moved."
  • This means: Before she moved (past event), she already had a pet (an even earlier past state).
  • Timeline: Had a pet ➝ Moved away

The past perfect "had had" places one past event before another past event in time.

Understanding the difference between stative and dynamic (action) verbs is essential for correct grammar:

  • Stative verbs describe states: be, have, know, want, believe, seem, own, love
  • Dynamic/action verbs describe actions: run, jump, eat, write, sing, play
  • Stative verbs cannot usually form the progressive (-ing) tense
  • Dynamic verbs freely use all tenses including progressive

Test: Can you add "-ing" naturally? If not, it's likely stative. "I am loving" sounds wrong, but "I love" sounds right — so "love" is stative.

The future perfect state uses "will have been" or "will have had". It describes a state that will be completed before a certain point in the future.

  • "By next year, she will have been a teacher for 10 years."
  • "By graduation, she will have had three different teachers."
  • Negative: "She won't have been happy about the results."
  • Question: "Will she have been ready by then?"
