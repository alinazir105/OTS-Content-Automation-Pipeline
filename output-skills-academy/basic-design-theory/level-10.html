THE CONCEPT

Design for People,
Not for Awards

Human-centered design means the people who will use something are at the center of every decision — not trends, not the designer's preferences, not cost-cutting. It asks: who are we designing for, what do they need, and does this actually work for them?

"Design that is hard to use isn't clever. It's a failure dressed up in aesthetics."

HUMAN-CENTERED DESIGN

Start with the person. Understand their real context — not an imagined ideal user. What devices do they have? What's their literacy level? Are they tired, distracted, stressed?

ACCESSIBILITY

About 1 in 7 people on Earth lives with some form of disability. Visual, motor, cognitive, hearing. Accessible design isn't a special case — it's good design for everyone.

INCLUSIVE DESIGN

Accessibility solves for specific impairments. Inclusive design asks a wider question: who is excluded by our assumptions? Age, language, culture, technology access.

THE CURB CUT EFFECT

Curb cuts were made for wheelchair users. They also help parents with strollers, delivery workers, injured people, cyclists. Designing for the edge benefits everyone.

JUDGE IT

Good Design vs. Bad Design for People

Look at each pair. Tap the card you think demonstrates BETTER human-centered design. Then read why.

ROUND 1 — BUTTON DESIGN

OPTION A

Large, high-contrast button with clear label. 56px height. Sufficient padding. One clear action.

OPTION B

Small, low-contrast button with tiny text. Hard to tap on mobile. Vague label. Poor color ratio.

ROUND 2 — ERROR MESSAGE

OPTION A

Vague error. No indication of which field failed or how to fix it. Color-only signal — inaccessible.

OPTION B

Specific error. Tells you exactly which field, what's wrong, and how to fix it. Icon + text signal.

ROUND 3 — ICON ONLY VS LABELLED

OPTION A

Icons with no text labels. Users must guess meaning. Fails for new users, elderly users, and cultural variation.

OPTION B

Icons paired with labels. Anyone can understand. Accessible, clear, universal — better for every user.

ACCESSIBILITY AUDIT TOOL

Accessibility Checklist

Use this checklist on any design — including your own portfolio pieces from previous levels. Check each item as you verify it. Save your score to your portfolio.

Colour contrast ratio is at least 4.5:1 Text is readable against its background in all states.
Information is not conveyed by color alone Errors, warnings, and status use icons or text — not just red/green.
Touch targets are at least 48×48px Buttons and links are large enough to tap accurately on mobile.
Text is legible at 16px or larger Body text is never smaller than 16px. Small text is used only for non-essential labels.
Error messages explain how to fix the error Not just "Error" — tell the user specifically what went wrong and what to do.
Labels are visible, not just placeholders Form fields have labels that remain visible when text is entered.
Language is plain and simple Jargon, abbreviations, and long legal sentences are avoided or explained.
Design works without internet / in slow conditions Loading states, error states, and offline states are considered.
