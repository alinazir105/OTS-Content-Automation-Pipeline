Core Concept بنیادی تصور

Two Ways to Search

Both methods find the same answer. But one is dramatically faster when the list is sorted. Here's how they think differently.

🔦 Linear Search

Start at the beginning. Check each item. Move to the next. Repeat until you find it — or reach the end. Simple, always works, but can be slow.

  • 1 Start at position 1
  • 2 Check: is this the one?
  • 3 If yes → found! Stop.
  • 4 If no → move to next item
  • 5 Repeat until end of list
⚠ Worst case: check EVERY item
List: [3, 7, 12, 19, 24, 31, 45]
Target: 31

Check 3 → ✗
Check 7 → ✗
Check 12 → ✗
Check 19 → ✗
Check 24 → ✗
Check 31 → ✓ Found! (6 steps)

⚡ Binary Search

The list must be sorted first. Then: check the middle item. If your target is smaller, ignore the right half. If larger, ignore the left half. Repeat — eliminating half each time.

  • 1 Find the middle item
  • 2 Check: equal, smaller, or larger?
  • 3 If equal → found! Stop.
  • 4 If smaller → eliminate right half
  • 5 If larger → eliminate left half
✓ 100 items → max 7 steps!
List: [3, 7, 12, 19, 24, 31, 45]
Target: 31

Middle = 19 → too small, go right
Middle = 31 → ✓ Found! (2 steps)

6 steps → 2 steps. 3× faster.
🕵️

The Detective's Secret

Binary Search is how a detective thinks. They don't check every suspect one by one. They ask smart questions that eliminate half the possibilities at once: "Was the crime committed before midnight?" Yes or No — and half the timeline is gone.

The catch: Binary Search only works when the list is already sorted. You can't check "the middle" if you don't know what order things are in. This is why sorting (Level 6) and searching (Level 7) are partners.

🕵️ Advanced Detective Thinking

Four hard questions that connect searching to the real world. Think before you click.

Why can Binary Search only work on sorted data?
🤔 Think about it: what assumption does Binary Search make at every step?
Binary Search works by checking the middle and then deciding "go left or go right." This only makes sense if you know that everything on the left is smaller and everything on the right is larger. Without sorting, "left" and "right" mean nothing — you can't eliminate half if the halves aren't meaningful. It's like asking "Is your suspect taller or shorter than the midpoint?" when everyone is standing randomly rather than in height order.
If you had 1,000,000 contacts, how many steps does Binary Search need at most?
💡 Hint: each step halves the remaining list. How many times can you halve 1,000,000 before reaching 1?
At most 20 steps. Because 2²⁰ = 1,048,576 > 1,000,000. Halve 1,000,000 → 500,000 → 250,000 → 125,000 → ... → 1. That's 20 halvings. Meanwhile, Linear Search might need to check all one million contacts. Binary Search is 50,000× more efficient in the worst case. This is called logarithmic time — one of the most powerful concepts in computer science.
Could you use Binary Search to find a word in a dictionary? Why or why not?
📖 Hint: think about what you actually do when you open a dictionary to look up a word...
Yes — and in fact, that's exactly what you already do without realising it. When you look up "zebra," you open near the back. If you land on "S," you know to go further right. If you land on "Z" but too far, you go left. Every time you open to a new page, you're eliminating half (or more) of the remaining pages. Dictionaries are sorted alphabetically precisely so this works. You're a human Binary Search engine every time you use one.
When might Linear Search be BETTER than Binary Search?
🔄 Consider: what if the list is unsorted? What if it only has 3 items? What if sorting would take longer than searching?
Three situations: 1. Unsorted data — if the list isn't sorted and sorting it would be expensive, Linear Search might be faster overall than sort-then-binary. 2. Very small lists — for 3 items, linear is simpler and equally fast. The overhead of calculating midpoints isn't worth it. 3. Streaming data — if items are arriving one at a time (like a Karachi traffic camera feed), you can't sort in advance. You check as things arrive. The best algorithm always depends on the specific situation — good thinkers choose tools wisely.
