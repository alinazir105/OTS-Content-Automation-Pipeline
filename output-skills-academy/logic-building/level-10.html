Core Concept

The Architecture of Reasoning

Every argument, decision, and belief rests on logic. Understanding how reasoning works — and where it breaks — gives you a superpower others don't have.

🔽

Deductive Reasoning

Starts with a general truth (called a premise) and works down to a specific, certain conclusion. If the premises are true and the logic is valid, the conclusion must be true.

Premise 1: All humans need water to survive.
Premise 2: Zara is a human.
Conclusion: ∴ Zara needs water to survive.
🔼

Inductive Reasoning

Starts with specific observations and builds up to a probable general conclusion. The conclusion isn't guaranteed — it's a best guess based on evidence. Science runs on induction.

Observation 1: The canteen queue was long on Monday.
Observation 2: The queue was long last Monday too.
Conclusion: ≈ Mondays probably have long queues.
Logical Fallacies

Arguments That Sound Right But Aren't

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning. Knowing these makes you harder to fool — and better at arguing well.

🥤 Straw Man
Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack.
"You said we should eat less junk food." → "So you want to ban all food? That's ridiculous!"
🔗 False Cause (Post Hoc)
Assuming that because A happened before B, A must have caused B.
"I wore my lucky shirt and we won the match. My shirt won the game!"
🏃 Hasty Generalisation
Drawing a broad conclusion from too few examples.
"Two students from that school misbehaved. All students from there must be trouble."
👥 Appeal to Popularity
Claiming something is true because many people believe it.
"Millions of people can't be wrong!" (They can be — the Earth was once 'flat'.)
👤 Ad Hominem
Attacking the person making the argument, not the argument itself.
"You can't trust her opinion on cricket — she failed PE last year."

Argument Analysis

Think Deeper

Spot the Hidden Fallacy

These scenarios are trickier. The fallacy is subtle — read carefully and choose which type of flawed reasoning is at work. Explain your thinking.

Scenario A: "Scientists have been saying for years that climate change is real. But Dr. Farhan — who has a PhD in chemistry — says the data is exaggerated. So really, we just don't know who's right."

✓ False Balance — This fallacy makes one minority voice sound equal to overwhelming scientific consensus. One PhD doesn't cancel thousands of studies. This is common in media and dangerous in decision-making.

Scenario B: "Every time Ali revises the night before an exam, he passes. He revised last night, so he will definitely pass tomorrow."

✓ Inductive Leap — This is inductive reasoning used too confidently. Past outcomes inform probability, but they don't guarantee future results. The word "definitely" is the giveaway — induction never gives certainty.

Scenario C: "You're saying we should reduce screen time for students. That's basically saying technology is evil and we should go back to the Stone Age."

✓ Straw Man — "Reduce screen time" was twisted into "ban all technology forever." This is a classic straw man: replace the real argument with an absurd extreme, then attack the extreme. Watch for this in debates.
Real-World Connection

Where Reasoning Rules Everything

Every field — law, science, business, daily conversations — runs on logic. Understanding reasoning makes you better at all of them.

⚖️

Law & Courts

Lawyers build deductive arguments: General laws apply to specific facts, leading to conclusions. A barrister must prove their argument is valid — not just believable. Fallacies get cases thrown out.

Deductive reasoning in action
🔬

Science & Research

Scientists use inductive reasoning: observe many cases, form a hypothesis, test it. But they stay humble — a single contradicting observation can overturn years of conclusions.

Inductive reasoning + humility
🗳️

Daily Decisions

From choosing what to eat to evaluating news headlines — we use reasoning constantly. Spotting fallacies in social media posts, advertisements, and political speeches is a life skill.

Critical thinking every day
🏏

Cricket Selection

Selectors can't say "he's great because we like him." They use innings data (induction) to form general trends, then apply deductive rules to specific selection criteria. Logic beats favouritism.

Karachi — evidence-based selection
